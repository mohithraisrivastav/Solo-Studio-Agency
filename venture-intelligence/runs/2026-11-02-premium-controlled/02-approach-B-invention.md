## 1. Problem framing

There is no problem statement. The job is to find physical objects or technologies that:
- a buyer would pay Rs 5 to 10 lakh per piece for (premium track) `[A]`, because a cheaper object cannot give the same reason to own it: scarcity, provenance, a working principle you can see or hear, a story tied to place or culture;
- or a business would pay for (technology track), by moving a mechanism from one field to another.

What "better" means here: for premium, a reason to buy that survives the question "why not a cheaper object?" (a visible principle, a numbered edition, a commissioned piece, a co-owned asset). For technology, a measurable gain for a business (rejects caught, time saved).

Constraints: capital Rs 0 `[A]` (from the task); one founder; no excluded industries from the brief; no prototypes or buyer contact in this run. Funding must come from buyers (deposit on commission, pre-order), a partner, or a licence.

Buyers' decision criteria I assume `[A]` (test: ask 5 galleries, architects, watch retailers what decides a Rs 5 to 10 lakh purchase): authenticity and scarcity; a maker with a name; installation that does not embarrass the buyer; after-sales care.

## 2. Inherited assumptions

Assumptions that nearly every existing product in these spaces shares. Each is a lever.

1. Decorative objects are made once, then sold as finished goods. (Lever: sell a commission or a numbered edition with deposit.)
2. A clock or watch shows civil time and the Gregorian calendar; Indian calendars are added by electronics or not at all. (Lever: mechanical lunar-day display.)
3. Glass art is judged by colour and form, not by the image the light makes. (Lever: design the light, not the glass.)
4. Noise control hides behind solid walls or foam. (Lever: a screen that is a pattern, letting light and air through.)
5. Telescope mirrors are glass with a thin coating. (Lever: go back to a cast metal mirror as a heritage object.)
6. Authenticity is a paper certificate or a tag attached to the object. (Lever: the object's own physical response is the certificate.)
7. One owner per object. (Lever: co-owned sets.)
8. Quality inspection of ceramics is a person tapping and listening. (Lever: a measured acoustic signature.)
9. Premium India-themed products are Swiss or foreign brands with Indian motifs. (Lever: India-made with the Indian principle inside.)

### P1. Panchang mechanical table clock (method: mechanism transfer)

**What it is.** A desk or mantel clock, numbered edition of 12 per year `[A]`, whose dials show civil time, the tithi (lunar day), the paksha, and the nakshatra using purely mechanical gearing, with no battery or electronics.

**Principle.** Tithi is the moon-sun angular separation in 12-degree steps. Tithis are uneven in length because the moon's speed varies. I computed the error of three gear-train levels over 30 years of sampled dates [calc, Meeus-style low-order lunar terms `[K]`]:
- Constant-speed (mean) gear train: median tithi-time error 8.0 h, 95th percentile 15.9 h, maximum 19.2 h `[I]`.
- Add one cam for the moon's main irregularity: median 2.8 h, 95th percentile 6.6 h, maximum 8.3 h `[I]`.
- Add cams for two more terms: median 3.0 h, 95th percentile 4.4 h, maximum 4.8 h `[I]`.
Gear-train exactness for the mean lunation also needs a high tooth count; a US patent shows the problem and one device to avoid it [S5, unverified at source].
A different route is a replaceable programmed cam drum for each year, set from an almanac, which gives minute-level accuracy but is not purely "self-computing". That is a design choice for the unknown below.

**Materials.** Brass and German silver plates, hardened steel arbors, hand-engraved dials in enamel or metal, optionally stone case. A sapphire or a plain mineral-glass cover. Movement parts could be machined by a contract shop (Rs 3 lakh unit cost `[A]`, test: three quotes).

**Intended buyer.** Indian watch collectors and families who observe the Panchang (festival days, auspicious timing), temple trusts and hotels as display pieces. Spending evidence (L1): Jacob & Co's Ram Mandir edition was priced at Rs 34 lakh, with 49 pieces made and 35 reported out of the shelf in 2024 [S18]; a later Rs 56 lakh edition of 10 pieces per design was reported in April 2025 [S18]. Titan's Edge Mechanical limited edition of 200 pieces was priced at Rs 1,95,000 [S19]. These show buyers pay lakhs for India-themed mechanical objects. They do not show anyone pays for a mechanical Panchang display.

**Distinguishing features.** Mechanical only; true (not mean) lunar motion shown; location-independent for tithi and nakshatra; numbered; service covenant (concept 19) attached.

**Potential commercial value.** Price Rs 8 lakh `[A]`, 10 units per year `[A]`, unit cost Rs 3 lakh `[A]`, fixed cost Rs 3 lakh per year `[A]`, 250 founder hours per unit `[A]`. Base case: gross margin Rs 5 lakh per unit, yearly surplus Rs 47 lakh, Rs 1,880 per founder hour `[I from calc]`. Halving units: Rs 22 lakh, Rs 1,760 per hour. Cost up 50%: Rs 32 lakh, Rs 1,280 per hour. Halving price: Rs 7 lakh surplus, Rs 280 per hour. Price halved, units halved, costs up 50% together: loss of Rs 5.5 lakh `[I from calc]`. Verdict: Unknown. Driven by the price a collector will pay for an unknown maker (halving price collapses the founder's hourly return).

**Funding route and first 10 buyers.** Edition of 12 sold from drawings and a cam-mechanism demonstration film with 50% deposit (Rs 4 lakh per piece `[A]`, which covers the parts at Rs 3 lakh `[A]`), so the first piece is funded by its first buyer. Alternative: licence the cam train to an existing Indian maker. Categories for the first 10: buyers of Indian-themed limited watches through Ethos Watches (the retailer of the Jacob & Co India editions [S18]); temple and trust committees (the Vikramaditya Vedic Clock was installed at Kashi Vishwanath and unveiled in 2025 [S3], so institutions do commission Panchang timekeeping); family offices; heritage hotels; independent watch makers as co-builders. Unverified: whether any of these will deposit for an unseen product.

**Single most important unknown.** Whether a buyer who cares about the Panchang accepts a mechanical display that is accurate to about 5 hours (cam train) or needs a yearly cam change for minute accuracy. Test: show the error table and a drawing to 5 Panchang-observant collectors or temple astrologers and ask what they would accept `[H]`.

### P2. Caustic window panel (method: research application)

**What it is.** A cast and polished glass panel, about 1 m by 1 m `[A]`, whose surface is calculated so that sunlight passing through forms a chosen image (a portrait, a landscape, a line of verse) on the floor or wall of the room. The glass looks clear or gently rippled; the image appears when the sun hits it.

**Principle.** Optimal-transport design of a free-form refractive surface so refracted light paints a target irradiance on a receiver [S8, S9, via search summary]. EPFL researchers (Pauly, Eigensatz, Bompas and others) presented the idea for windows at Glass Performance Days 2013 in Tampere [S8]. The patent family covers refractive glass windows that project sunlight onto a wall or floor [S9, unverified at source].

**Physics I checked [calc].** The sun is a disc 0.533 degrees wide `[K]`. At a throw (panel to floor) of 2 m the edge blur is 1.9 cm `[I]`. With a 20-degree maximum ray deflection `[A]` the image is 1.46 m wide, so about 78 resolvable elements across `[I]`; with a 10-degree deflection, about 38 across `[I]`. That is enough for a bold portrait or large glyphs, not for fine photographs. The image drifts about 0.52 m per hour at 2 m throw near normal incidence `[I]` (sun moves 15 degrees per hour `[K]`), so it stays legible only while the sun is near the design angle, around one to two hours a day `[I]`. A moving-day effect (the image slides across the floor) can be sold as a feature.

**Materials.** Cast or CNC-ground borosilicate or float glass, optionally acrylic for a first test. Steel frame.

**Intended buyer.** Architects and interior designers for villas, hotels, spas, museums, libraries; collectors of light art; memorial rooms.

**Distinguishing features.** The artwork is on the floor, made of the sun; no electricity; each piece is calculated from a client image so no two are alike; the image can carry a name, a poem, a temple; installation includes a sun-path study for the site.

**Potential commercial value.** Price Rs 7 lakh `[A]`, 8 per year `[A]`, unit cost Rs 2.5 lakh `[A]`, fixed cost Rs 2 lakh `[A]`, 150 founder hours per piece `[A]`. Base: gross margin Rs 4.5 lakh per unit, surplus Rs 34 lakh, Rs 2,833 per hour `[I from calc]`. Units halved: Rs 16 lakh. Cost up 50%: Rs 24 lakh. Price halved: Rs 6 lakh surplus, Rs 500 per hour. All adverse: loss Rs 3 lakh `[I from calc]`. Verdict: Unknown. Driven by price a buyer will pay and glass-grinding cost; neither is sourced. Hotel art commissions are real in India (St. Regis Mumbai lobby mural, Park Hyatt Hyderabad sculpture [S26]); no budget figures found in 1 search.

**Funding route and first 10 buyers.** Pre-sale: 50% deposit on a commission from an architect or hospitality designer, with a simulated image of the caustic for their site `[H]`. The design software is the asset; the first physical piece is the first paid commission, made first in acrylic as a test, then glass. Legal path: licence from EPFL (applicant of US10732405 [S9]) or confirm freedom to operate. First 10 buyer categories: architecture and interior firms doing luxury villas; boutique hotel and spa developers; museum and library architects; private galleries; glass artists who want a design service. No licensee or spin-off was found in 1 search `[S9]`.

**Single most important unknown.** Whether the projected image stays legible and attractive under a real sun (disc blur, drifting angle, atmospheric haze, a typical Indian site's sun path). Cheapest test: a CNC-cut acrylic panel from a simulated design and one day outdoors, before any quote. Second-order risk: patent freedom to operate (patent runs to about 2034 to 2035 by my inference, not checked `[S9]`).

### P3. Acoustic jali (method: mechanism transfer)

**What it is.** A freestanding screen, about 2 m wide by 2.4 m high `[A]`, in stone, brass or bronze, with a lattice pitch designed as a sonic crystal to cut a chosen band of noise while looking like a traditional jali. It lets air and light through.

**Principle.** A periodic array of scatterers in air blocks sound near the Bragg frequency c divided by twice the pitch [S12]. From the formula [calc, sound speed 343 m/s `[K]`]: 4 cm pitch gives a gap near 4,288 Hz, 8 cm near 2,144 Hz, 15 cm near 1,143 Hz, 30 cm near 572 Hz, 60 cm near 286 Hz `[I]`. Restaurant clatter and speech sit around 1 to 4 kHz `[K]`, so pitches of 4 to 15 cm suit; low-frequency generator hum and traffic rumble (under 500 Hz `[K]`) would need pitches of 35 cm or more, which does not look like a jali.

**Materials.** Cast brass or bronze rods, hand-cut Jaisalmer or Dholpur stone, or ceramic. Helmholtz-resonator inserts (hollow cylindrical shells with slots) can widen the gap [S11].

**Intended buyer.** Architects and owners of courtyard restaurants, hotel gardens, spas, embassies, luxury villas next to roads, recording rooms.

**Distinguishing features.** Visible craft; designed attenuation band declared per piece; measured insertion loss certificate from an accredited lab (EN 1793-5 and -6 style tests were used for a Valencia barrier [S11]).

**Potential commercial value.** Price Rs 6 lakh `[A]`, 10 per year `[A]`, unit cost Rs 2.5 lakh `[A]`, fixed cost Rs 2 lakh `[A]`, 120 hours `[A]`. Base surplus Rs 33 lakh, Rs 2,750 per hour `[I from calc]`; price halved: Rs 3 lakh surplus, Rs 250 per hour; all adverse: loss Rs 5.8 lakh `[I from calc]`. Verdict: Unknown. Driven by whether buyers value a few decibels.

**Evidence that hurts.** A field study (Qin et al., 2022) found sonic crystal barriers beat concrete barriers by only 0.5 to 2.1 dB(A) inside the band gap, and concrete was better overall at every distance [S11, via search summary]. A 2024 full-scale PVC-tube test reported maximum noise reduction of 20.98 dB but insertion loss of only 2.5 dB(A) [S11]. A 2026 lab study predicted peak A-weighted transmission loss of 45.8 dB but measured 10.1 dB [S11]. A review warned that a band gap alone is not enough for an effective barrier [S12]. So this is a designed filter and a beautiful screen, not a noise wall.

**Funding route and first 10 buyers.** A university acoustics lab or the stone-craft guild co-funds a test panel (partner-funded); then architect commissions with deposit. First 10 buyer categories: architects who already specify jali; boutique hotels; restaurant groups with courtyards; embassies. None contacted.

**Single most important unknown.** Whether a craft-scale lattice achieves at least about 3 dB(A) of real insertion loss in the target noise (3 dB is the smallest change people reliably notice `[K]`) without a solid wall. Test: build one 1 m by 1 m brass-rod panel and measure with a calibrated meter before any sale.

### P4. Speculum telescope (method: constraint change)

**What it is.** A numbered edition of 25 `[A]` reflecting telescopes (150 to 200 mm aperture `[A]`) whose primary mirror is a hand-cast, hand-figured copper-tin bell-metal mirror (the same family of alloy as 17th to 19th century telescopes), in a brass and teak tube. It is a working instrument and a heritage object.

**Principle.** Speculum is a copper-tin alloy of roughly 68% copper to 32% tin, hard to cast and prone to tarnish, but it reflects from the metal surface directly [S2]. The Aranmula mirror of Kerala is a front-surface metal mirror; a 2020 arXiv study tested an Aranmula speculum mirror (reflectivity near infrared to extreme ultraviolet; visible figure not seen) [S21, unverified at source]. Hobbyists and projects have cast 2 to 6 inch speculum blanks at Herschel's ratio [S2], and Japanese school and amateur groups reported a copper-tin 7:3 casting with polishing difficulty and low reflectivity [S20]. My arithmetic [calc]: with reflectivity 0.65 `[K]` (aluminised glass 0.88 `[K]`), a speculum mirror collects 0.74 of the light `[I]`: a 200 mm mirror gives 204 cm² of effective area against 276 cm² `[I]`.

**Materials.** Cast bell-metal blank, hand-ground and polished, optional protective clear coating (a coating reduces the "pure metal" claim), brass, teak, steel.

**Intended buyer.** Collectors of scientific instruments, astronomy-minded wealthy families, luxury resorts with observatories, schools and science museums, Kerala heritage institutions.

**Distinguishing features.** The mirror itself is the story: cast, not coated; maker's name; Kerala metallurgy; numbered; each sold with a first-light record.

**Potential commercial value.** Price Rs 6 lakh `[A]`, 8 per year `[A]`, unit cost Rs 2 lakh `[A]`, fixed Rs 1.5 lakh `[A]`, 200 hours `[A]`. Base surplus Rs 30.5 lakh, Rs 1,906 per hour `[I from calc]`; price halved Rs 6.5 lakh surplus; all adverse loss Rs 1.5 lakh `[I from calc]`. Price anchors (all weak): a made-to-order handmade brass Newtonian at USD 4,500 and up, about Rs 3.8 lakh `[A]` [S22]; a large brass Newtonian resale at USD 7,800, about Rs 6.6 lakh `[A]` [S22]. Rs 6 lakh is at the top of what these show. Verdict: Unknown. Driven by price and by whether a castable, figurable blank exists.

**Funding route and first 10 buyers.** Co-development with an Aranmula workshop (the makers' society registers about 18 authorised units [S1]); a science museum or heritage hotel pays a deposit for the first instrument. First 10 buyer categories: collectors of scientific instruments; resort observatories; museums; the Vishwabrahmana metal mirror society as partner not buyer. Unverified: any buyer.

### T1. Acoustic fingerprint certificate (technology track, method: mechanism transfer)

**What it is.** A measurement service and phone-readable method that records the ring-down spectrum (mode frequencies and decay rates) of a hand-cast bell, glass piece or ceramic, and stores it as that object's identity. The business buying it: makers, galleries, and auctioneers of limited-edition crafts, and a feature for P1 to P4.

**Principle.** Every hand-made object has a slightly different set of resonances; this works like a physical unclonable function. Mode frequency scales with the square root of the elastic modulus, so a temperature rise of 10 K shifts it about 0.2% (0.5 × 4×10⁻⁴ per K × 10 K × 100, with the modulus temperature coefficient assumed `[A]`) [calc], about 2 Hz at 1 kHz `[I]`.

**Buyer, price, funding.** Makers pay about Rs 2,000 per certified piece `[A]`; 200 pieces a year gives Rs 4 lakh a year `[I from calc]`, too small alone. Funding: free pilot with one maker in return for a letter and a fee schedule; a licence to a larger authenticity platform.

**Single most important unknown.** Whether two measurements of the same piece (different temperature, mounting, wear) match more closely than two different pieces of the same pattern. Test: ring 10 identical-pattern bowls 5 times each across a temperature range and plot within-piece against between-piece spread.

Queries: "acoustic physical unclonable function bell ring resonance spectrum authentication object patent"; "Alitheon FeaturePrint luxury goods authentication unique surface microstructure handcrafted objects Entrupy price". Non-English: Chinese "声学指纹 共振频谱 防伪 钟 瓷器 无源 身份识别 专利".
Near neighbours: Lexmark US11522694B2 acoustical PUF made from particle-in-binder object, priority February 2020 [S14]; Nokia US10491407B2 photoacoustic PUF, expired [S14]; CSIC acousto-optic PUF, licensing offer [S14]; "Listening to disorder" preprint (audio PUF) [S14]; US6724689 acoustic resonance identification of body parts [search result]; Alitheon FeaturePrint, optical, used in luxury goods and collectibles [S15]; Entrupy, AI plus microscopy, USD 119 per month and USD 24 per authentication (pages disagree) [S15].
Real difference: using the natural ring spectrum of an unmodified cast or ceramic piece rather than an engineered particle object or optical image. Not found in 6 queries, but crowded neighbours (Alitheon, Entrupy) make this a feature, not a stand-alone business.

### T2. Tap-tone grader for Morbi ceramics (technology track, method: mechanism transfer)

**What it is.** A bench or conveyor station that taps a tile or sanitary item with a calibrated hammer, listens, and flags cracked pieces by comparing the spectrum to a reference. The mechanism comes from luthiers and bell founders (listening to modes).

**Honest note.** This is an adaptation of an existing industrial method, not a new invention. Acoustic resonance testing is established (Resoniks QCFlex uses a tapping hammer and deep learning for pass/fail [S16]; EP0636881 describes a sound test for ceramic articles including roof tiles [S16]; Anadolu University paper "Resonant Inspection of Ceramic Tiles" [S16]; Fraunhofer IZFP paper on green sanitary ceramics, 2014 [S16]).

**Buyer, price, funding.** Morbi has 800 or more tile factories within about 60 km [S17, vendor claim]. Price per station Rs 6 lakh `[A]`; if one station replaces two inspectors at Rs 20,000 per month each `[A]`, the saving is Rs 4.8 lakh per year and payback 1.25 years `[I from calc]`. The wage and crack-loss numbers are assumptions (test: ask three factories for inspector headcount and crack returns). Funding: a pilot installation at one factory with a pre-order fee; no hardware before that.

**Single most important unknown.** Whether glazed, odd-shaped tiles and sanitary ware give a clean good/bad separation at line speed (false rejects) in a factory with noisy ambient sound. Test: record taps on 100 good and 100 cracked pieces from one factory and cluster offline.

## 6. Wildcard: co-owned shruti-tuned bell set (method: business model shift)

**What it is.** A set of 12 `[A]` cast bronze bells, each tuned to a chosen pitch of an Indian scale (for example 12 of the 22 shruti `[K]`), each held by a different owner (families in a building, a temple's patrons, a campus). The set only sounds as a scale when the owners bring bells together; each owner has a numbered bell and a vote on how the set is played.

**Why it might work.** Named-bell sponsorship already sells at Rs 5 to 10 lakh levels outside India: a UK parish offers a bell for GBP 11,000 (about Rs 12.1 lakh `[A]`) or GBP 6,000 shared (about Rs 6.6 lakh `[A]`) [S23]; a Flemish abbey offers bell godparenthood at EUR 3,500 (about Rs 3.3 lakh `[A]`) for one of 12 smaller bells [S23]; a US campaign sells named bells at USD 35,000 to USD 100,000 [S23]. These are donations, not retail sales, and show people pay to be attached to a bell.

**Funding.** Founder sells 12 shares with deposits; the foundry casts after 12 deposits are in. Cash need before first revenue: zero if all 12 pay a deposit first; the risk is that fewer than 12 sign up.

**Single most important unknown.** Whether 12 unrelated owners will co-own a shared object with governance rules, and whether a foundry can tune bronze bells to shruti pitches within a few cents `[H]`. Test: ask one foundry whether it can tune and at what cost; ask one housing society committee whether they would pay.

**Prior-art search (3 queries).** "carillon bells shared ownership each bell sponsored..." (sponsorship exists); English "tuned bell set just intonation shruti 22 microtones..." (no shruti-tuned bell sets found; shruti boxes and bowls only); Dutch "klokkengieterij beiaard klokken zuivere stemming kleine klokkenspel particulier kopen prijs per klok" (foundries Eijsbouts and Petit & Fritsen named; no per-bell retail price found). Not claimed original; only 3 searches, below the 8 the standard requires.
