## 1. Steelman

India has thousands of temples, trusts and estates, and large bells are a recognised gift: one 2,400 kg ashtadhatu bell was donated to the Ayodhya Ram temple for Rs 25 lakh [S5]. Foundries in Kerala and Tamil Nadu cast bronze bells [S3, S16], but nobody in India markets a bell whose pitch and partials are designed and verified. A tuned bell is a different object from a cast one, and a 12-bell set owned by 12 people gives each owner a claim to a shared instrument that cannot be bought whole. Buyer advances or a trust commission could fund the first piece with no founder capital.

## 3. The seven headings

### 3.1 Original
Five nearest, with price:
1. **Grassmayr (Austria), Eijsbouts (Netherlands), Paccard (France), Taylor (UK), Meneely (US)** cast tuned church and carillon bells. Tuning to strike note and partials is standard practice [S13, S2]. Price is quote-based and unpublished. A planning blog gives about USD 25,000 to 90,000 for a 250 to 800 lb bell and USD 75,000 to 250,000 for 1,000 to 3,000 lb; low reliability [S1]. A real data point: 16 new Eijsbouts carillon bells cost EUR 55,000 installed at Tartu, about EUR 3,400 each, and tuning 18 old bells cost EUR 15,000 [S9]. At EUR 3,400 a bell the European tuned product is cheaper per bell than the concept's Rs 5 to 10 lakh target [I: EUR 3,400 is roughly Rs 3 lakh at an assumed Rs 90 per EUR [A]], though those are small carillon bells and the quote includes installation.
2. **Malmark and Schulmerich handbell sets (US)**: tuned bronze bells, 25-note set USD 12,000 to 15,000 [S8]. A tuned multi-owner ensemble already exists as a product: choirs buy a set and each ringer holds one or two bells. Ownership is by the church, not the ringer.
3. **Chinese bianzhong makers (Suizhou)**: tuned bronze bell sets with two tones per bell; intangible-heritage records say Suizhou makers produce about 3,000 sets a year [S14]. No price found. Not found after 2 searches (queries: "编钟 复制 定音 价格 一套", "编钟 定制 报价").
4. **Japanese temple bell (bonsho) founders**: no price found. Not found after 1 search (query: "梵鐘 価格 鋳造 音 調律 寺 費用 万円"), which returned only heritage pages.
5. **Indian bell foundries**: Aics Bell Foundry (Vadakankulam, Tamil Nadu, founded 1925 per its listing) [S3, S16]; Madonna (Mannar, Kerala) [S16]; Jalesar (Etah) workshop [S5]; Kumbakonam makers (a 613 kg bronze bell reported) [S15]. Prices Rs 8.5 to 18 lakh for 500 to 1,000 kg [S3].

Also found: Whitechapel closed in 2017 citing rising costs and falling demand [S11]; a UK trust sought about GBP 5 million to save Loughborough [S11]. Bell founding is a shrinking business even where tuned bells are the norm.

What is different: a designed, tuned, small-batch bell sold with the designer's name in India, and co-ownership. A buyer would notice tuning only if they can hear it and care. Grade: **major**; the difference is real but small, and patents exist [S6, S7]. Novelty search: 17 searches [I: count of my queries] including German, Japanese, Chinese, Dutch (the Dutch and Japanese searches returned no prices). I did not search trademarks or crowdfunding listings beyond one query (no tuned-bell Kickstarter found; "Kickstarter tuned bronze bells").

### 3.2 Desired
Payer: a temple trust, institution, estate or garden owner; or a private donor (the Mittal brothers [S5]). Beneficiary: the congregation or visitors. Budget line: a donation or capital gift, not an operating budget. Behaviour shown: donors do pay Rs 21 to 25 lakh for a very large bell [S4, S5]; donors giving Rs 5 to 10 lakh to the Ayodhya fund numbered 1,428 [S17], but that is cash for construction, not bells.

Why pay for tuning? Sources describe tuning as what makes a bell sound clean (inner harmony) and say a church inspector checks it before acceptance [S13]. Evidence that Indian temple buyers ask for it: none found. Temple bells are valued for loudness and metal: the Ayodhya bell was reported with the claim it can be heard 10 km away [S5], and the ashtadhatu composition is the selling point [S5]. Tuning is a church and carillon virtue. Indian temple ghanta tradition prizes a long, resonant "Om" ring, which may be achievable without a designed partial series [H]. Cost of cheapest alternative: an off-the-shelf bell at Rs 1,700 to 2,000 per kg [S3, I].

Co-ownership: no evidence anyone wants it. Twelve people each paying Rs 5 to 10 lakh must also coordinate to meet. The instrument "exists only when owners bring bells together" [brief]. For a buyer this is a defect as much as a feature: the object they paid for is silent alone. Grade: **fatal for form (b) as a Rs 5 to 10 lakh product** [H].

### 3.3 Feasible
A tuned bell needs profile design (software or an experienced founder), a cast with allowance, then metal removal on a lathe. Technically possible; no India-specific proof. Whether any Indian foundry has a vertical lathe and a person who can read partials is unknown. Not found after 4 searches. Cost: see 3.4. Tuning by hand loses the bell if too much metal is removed; Chinese sources note over-filing makes the pitch flat and the bell is scrapped [S14, via search summary]. Experiment that settles it: ask one foundry to cast and tune one 20 to 40 kg bell and report measured partials (not run).

### 3.4 Profitable
Inputs: copper Rs 1,000 per kg [A: between Rs 590 scrap [S12] and Rs 1,330 MCX spot [S12]]; tin USD 52,913 per tonne [S12], converted at Rs 89 per USD [A]; melt loss 8% [A]; casting labour Rs 250 per kg [A]; tuning 40 hours at Rs 400 [A]; foundry margin 15% [A]; design 60 hours and sale 60 hours at Rs 750 [A]. Full list in the script.

Base case, Rs 7.5 lakh ex-GST, 300 kg: metal Rs 1,974 per kg [I]; foundry price to founder about Rs 8.0 lakh [I]; net after founder time **negative Rs 1.78 lakh** [I]. GST excluded; add on top for buyer [A].

Grid (net after founder time, Rs): at Rs 5 lakh, 150 kg: negative Rs 44,000; at Rs 7.5 lakh, 150 kg: positive Rs 2.06 lakh; at Rs 10 lakh, 250 kg: positive Rs 2.0 lakh; 350 kg or more loses at Rs 7.5 lakh [I, script]. So the idea pays only if the bell is small (about 150 to 250 kg) AND the buyer pays Rs 7.5 to 10 lakh for it. At 150 kg an ordinary market bell sells for about Rs 2.6 to 3.0 lakh [I: 150 kg at Rs 1,700 to 2,000 per kg [S3]]. So the tuning premium to be believed is about 2.5 to 3 times.

Sensitivity (each unfavourable by half, one at a time, 300 kg base) [I]: the sign never flips because the base is already negative; the largest swing is copper at Rs 1,500 per kg (net negative Rs 3.2 lakh [I]), then foundry margin and casting labour [I, script]. At the favourable 150 kg, Rs 7.5 lakh point, net is Rs 2.06 lakh before the unsourced inputs are stressed. Verdict driven by weight and price premium, both unsourced: "Unknown. Driven by whether a buyer pays 2.5 to 3 times the metal price for tuning."

Note: the Ayodhya bell sold at about Rs 1,000 per kg [S4, S5, I], which is below my metal cost of Rs 1,974 per kg [I]. Either that bell used a cheaper alloy (it is ashtadhatu, and the reports differ on weight and cost [S4, S5]) or my alloy cost is too high. This inconsistency would improve my numbers if my costs are 40% high; I did not stress it. At Rs 1,000 per kg metal cost the 300 kg base would roughly break even; it would not reach founder rate [I, not run].

### 3.6 Defensible
Patents exist already for designed-partial bells [S6, S7]; they are US patents and I did not read their claims. What would accumulate: a tuned-bell reference library and a named acoustic designer. A foundry that learns to tune can sell to others directly. Defensibility is weak: the foundry holds the skill, the founder holds a brief. Grade: **major** [I].

### 3.7 Large enough, within means
Founder income target: 1,800 hours at Rs 750 is Rs 13.5 lakh a year [A: 1,800 hours]. At the favourable point (150 kg, Rs 7.5 lakh) net per bell is about Rs 2.06 lakh after 120 founder hours [I, script], which means about 6 to 7 bells a year to reach Rs 13.5 lakh, with unknown buyer supply and the founder doing all sale and design. At the 300 kg base every bell loses. Capital before first revenue: zero from the founder; the foundry would need a deposit for metal (about Rs 6 lakh of metal for 300 kg [I]) or the buyer advances it. Credibility of that funding route: no evidence a trust or donor prepays a first-of-kind bell from an unknown. [H].

Form (b): the set at 12 x 120 kg bells, Rs 7.5 lakh each, nets Rs 3.2 lakh per bell and Rs 38 lakh for the set [I, script]; those figures assume the set's design cost is shared and every bell sells [A], but only if all 12 buyers sign. One failure to fill the set and the instrument does not exist.

## 4. Objections

**Fatal**
- F1. No buyer evidence for co-ownership; it asks 12 strangers for Rs 60 lakh to 1.2 crore [I] for an object that is silent alone. [H]
- F2. At 300 kg and Rs 7.5 lakh the unit loses money; viable only at about 150 to 250 kg and a 2.5 to 3 times premium over market for tuning [I, S3]. [I]

**Major**
- M1. No count of buyers at Rs 5 to 10 lakh a year; only one documented donation [S4, S5].
- M2. No Indian foundry shown to tune; the one tuned Indian installation used French bells [S16]. 
- M3. Tuning is a church virtue; no evidence Indian temples value it [H].
- M4. Patents on designed-partial bells [S6, S7]; low copy cost.

**Minor**
- m1. Bell founding abroad shrank (Whitechapel closed 2017) [S11].
- m2. GST and installation cost not in buyer price [A].

## 5. What holds up
- Donors do buy very large bells for temples [S4, S5].
- Tuning is a real physical skill, and a tuned multi-owner ensemble is a product already (handbells) [S8].
- Indian foundries exist that can cast bells of 500 to 1,000 kg [S3].

## 6. Disagreements with specialists
I was told to read no other files. The brief says the large bells "documented (Rs 21 to 25 lakh)" are evidence of the price band. They are one object reported twice [S4, S5]; it is a single donation, not two comparables, and the price per kg is about half my metal cost.

## 7. Simpler alternatives
Sell an existing foundry bell at Rs 1,700 to 2,000 per kg [S3] as a broker for a fee of 5 to 10%: about Rs 40,000 to 80,000 on a Rs 8 lakh bell [I]. Or sell tuning as a service to the foundry (a measurement report and re-profile). Or sell the 12-set as a rented or institution-owned set, as handbell makers do [S8].

## 8. Verdict

**REJECT (as framed).**

Even-if-true test. Assume every open point resolves in favour: foundry can tune, temples value tuning, and some will pay a premium. Then: (a) at 150 kg and Rs 7.5 lakh, net is about Rs 2 lakh a bell [I], needing 6 to 7 bells a year from a buyer pool with one documented transaction and no list, each sale taking months and needing a first-of-kind prepayment; (b) needs 12 independent private buyers for one object. Headings 5 and 7 still fail for (b) and are doubtful for (a).

What would reopen (a) only: a single drop-in change, a 150 to 250 kg bell, sold to institutions. 

Decisive cheap experiment (not run now): ask two named trust [H] or estate decision-makers, by one conversation each, whether they would pay Rs 7.5 lakh for a 150 to 250 kg bell when a Rs 3 lakh untuned bell is available [I], and whether they have bought a bell in the last five years [H]. If neither would, stop. Second, one foundry quote for tuning measurement of a sample bell. Most important unknown: whether any Indian buyer pays 2.5 to 3 times metal price for tuning. Confidence in REJECT: medium, because no source was opened and my cost inputs are assumptions.

Unchecked: trademarks, patent claims in full, Japanese and Dutch prices, Kerala and Odisha (Kansari) bell-metal centres, GST rate on bells, Indian foundry capacity for lathe tuning.
