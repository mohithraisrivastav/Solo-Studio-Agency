# Invention 1: Capturing value from gold loss in Indian jewellery manufacturing

Specialist: Inventor. Date: 2026-10-31.

Constraints from the brief: founder capital ₹5,00,000 and founder time at ₹750 per hour [A] (basis: brief; test: none needed). Primary market India.

Method and limits.
- I ran 36 web searches. I did not try WebFetch, because the brief says it is blocked for most sites. I opened no pages. Every source I found myself is marked `search summary`.
- Sources taken from 03-market-1-gold-loss.md or 01-hunt-A.md are marked in the Sources table as "reported in 03 (or 01), not re-checked". I treat them as the investigator's evidence, not mine.
- I contacted no buyer. Nothing here is above demand level L1.
- Gold price: indicative 24K retail rates in October 2026 ran from about ₹14,280-14,320 per gram (26 October) to ₹14,902 per gram (7 October), excluding GST [S1, S2]. I use ₹14,300 per gram of fine gold [S1]. Every rupee value of gold below scales with this.
- USD converted at ₹88 per USD [A] (basis: approximate recent level, not checked; test against the RBI reference rate).
- Not checked: Indian refiner rate cards for sweeps (not found after 2 searches), polishing job-work prices (not found after 3 searches), CFC fee schedules (not found after 1 search), insurance premiums for gold in custody, and pollution-consent rules for waste handling that does not burn anything.

## 1. Problem framing

**The job, by buyer.**
- **Job-work workshop owner (Segment B).** Keep more of the gold allowance. In the one ruled case, the owner received 40 g per 1,000 g issued, sub-contracted at 30 g, and kept 10 g, which is 1% of the gold [S3]. Their gold margin is: allowance received, minus allowance paid to sub-karigars, minus net loss on work done by salaried staff, plus what they realise from selling waste [I] (from the ruling's structure [S3]).
- **In-house manufacturer (Segment C).** Cut net loss, meaning gross loss minus metal recovered. A listed manufacturer went from above 1.5% to about 0.5% loss [S5] and its broker credits about 100 bps of gross margin to this [S4].
- **What "better" means.** Net loss in basis points of gold throughput; realisation as a share of the fine gold contained in waste; days to settlement. One basis point on 1 kg is 0.1 g, worth ₹1,430 [I] (0.1 g × ₹14,300 [S1]).

**Where the money sits.**
- Waste grades differ by 40 times: clean filings about 40% fine gold, dirty bench sweeps 5-20%, buffing dust about 2%, floor sweeps about 1% (one US supplier's guide) [S9]. A Turkish thesis puts floor sweepings in small workshops at about 1.2% gold [S30]. One review gives polishing waste at 5.34% gold, from a thesis abstract [S31].
- Small workshops recover far less than factories. One small-artisan leader is quoted saying big manufacturers recover about 99% and small artisans 25-30% [S6]. This is one person's claim. A factory study cites a refinery recovering about 92% of gross loss on average [S7].
- Waste is already sold. Informal collectors (Kadaiwallas in Bengal, Nehariyas in north India) clean workshops and recover gold from dust [S10]. In Kolkata a contractor pays a workshop each month for the right to sweep it and takes away the mats [S12]; Peshawar collectors also pay workshops for their dirt [S13]. Some jewellers store dust for years and sell it in bulk for lakhs of rupees [S10]. One source says sweepings are sold 10-40% below market value [S14] (Hunter's source; the summary did not tie the figure to one URL).
- Refiners abroad pay 92-94% of assayed gold for sweeps lots, after a USD 75 assay fee (about ₹6,600 [A]) [S8].

**Constraints the buyer lives with.**
- Theft and fraud near the metal: gold dust worth ₹75 lakh stolen from an Andheri jeweller [S39]; a Bengaluru polishing firm's books showed 406.16 g of dust while the actual weight was 1,091.48 g [S38]; absconding karigars [S55].
- Karigars are scarce and non-exclusive; one IPO-bound maker had no formal deal with 12 of its 41 karigars [S51].
- No space: Zaveri Bazaar reportedly does not permit manufacturing equipment [S6].
- Informality and tax: gold sweepings carry 3% GST under HSN 71129920 (secondary sources) [S36]. A formal sale leaves a paper trail that a cash sale does not [I].
- Regulators are looking at gold labs: a joint committee told a tribunal that all assaying and hallmarking centres must obtain consent under the Water and Air Acts [S35].

**Buyer decision criteria** [A] (basis: the constraints above; test in 15 interviews): grams back per kg of throughput; no upfront cash; seeing their own metal weighed and sealed; no disruption to karigars; settlement speed; how much paper the deal creates.

**One contradiction to keep in view.** A Kolkata trade feature says Maharashtra craftsmen are allowed 1 g per 100 g but lose about 50 mg; the summary did not state the unit basis [S50]. If real loss is that small for some work, the pool for capture concepts is small there. I trust the listed manufacturer's 0.5% net loss after improvement [S5] more for factory work, because it comes from company disclosures reported by brokers. Confidence in the size of the pool: medium.

**Rough size of one buyer's pool.** Rajkot makes about 150 tonnes a year of handmade plain gold [S57] with about 50,000 plain-gold artisans [S58], which is about 250 g per artisan per month [I] (150,000 kg ÷ 50,000 ÷ 12). A 10-karigar workshop then handles about 2.5 kg a month and a 50-worker plant about 12.5 kg [A] (basis: the Rajkot ratio; machine-made plants run higher; test by asking 10 owners).

## 5. Three lead concepts

### Lead 1: Witnessed waste auction ("waste mandi")

**Method.** Mechanism transfer from two fields. (a) Mining concentrate and refining trade: seller and refiner each assay a split of the same sample, and a sealed umpire sample settles any gap. One refiner's published guideline sets out this umpire procedure [S23]. (b) Dairy collection centres in India test each farmer's milk at the collection point and pay by fat content, so the seller sees the measurement [K].

**Core mechanism.** Take the measurement away from the buyer. Today the collector or refiner who buys the waste also weighs and assays it [S10, S12]. The mandi weighs and seals the lot in front of the owner, has a split assayed by an independent lab, and makes licensed refiners compete on terms.

**How it works.**
1. The owner books a pickup. The lot is weighed on a certified balance in front of the owner, sealed in numbered tamper-evident bags, and filmed.
2. Licensed refiners [S52] bid on terms, not on a price: payable share of assayed fine gold, fixed charges, and days to pay. This is how sweeps are priced abroad [S8].
3. The winning refiner burns, mills, screens and melts the lot with our staff watching. Refiners normally burn sweeps before assay [K]. The sample is split three ways (refiner, owner, sealed umpire), following ISO 11596 sampling practice [S24].
4. An independent fire-assay lab tests the owner's split. BIS assaying centres already run fire assay on 300-500 mg samples [S33]. If the two assays differ beyond an agreed limit, the umpire sample decides [S23].
5. The refiner pays the owner directly, in fine gold credit or by bank transfer. We invoice our fee. No gold passes through our books, so we need no working capital in gold and hold no gold overnight [I].
6. The owner gets a lot history: fine gold per kg of throughput, by stream, against anonymised peers.

**What the buyer experiences.** "My bag was weighed and sealed while I watched. Three licensed refiners quoted. I have an assay certificate. I pay a fee only if I beat my last sale."

**Measurable advantage against the best current alternative** (selling an unassayed lot to a collector or one refiner).
- If the mandi nets the seller about 88% of contained gold (a 92% payable [S8] minus a 4% fee [A]), and the owner realises 60-85% today [A] (basis: the 10-40% discount in [S14] and the 25-30% recovery quote in [S6]; test below), the gain is 3-28 points of contained value [I].
- For a 10-karigar workshop at 2.5 kg a month [A] with 0.3-0.8% of throughput leaving as sold waste [A] (basis: gross loss of a few per cent, most of it filings melted back in-house; test by assaying 10 lots against throughput records), contained gold is 7.5-20 g a month, worth ₹1,07,250-2,86,000 [I]. A 10-point gain is worth ₹10,700-28,600 a month to that owner [I].
- At the top of the range (realisation already 85%), the gain is only 3 points and barely covers the fee [I]. The concept depends on realisation being well below 85%.

**Who pays, price logic, frequency.**
- The seller pays a success fee of 3-4% of contained value, charged only when the net beats their last reference price by at least 5 points [A] (basis: must leave most of the gain with the seller; test in the pilot).
- Lots come monthly to yearly. Owners clean before Diwali [S11] and some hold dust for years [S10].
- Revenue per workshop: ₹38,600-1,37,300 a year [I] (7.5-20 g × 12 × ₹14,300 × 3-4%). Fifty workshops in one cluster: about ₹19-69 lakh a year [I].
- Founder time: 50 workshops × 4 lots × 4 hours = 800 hours, worth ₹6,00,000 at ₹750 an hour [I].

**Production or delivery outline.** One cluster first (Kolkata Bowbazar or Rajkot). Kit: certified balance, tamper-evident bags, body camera, sample splitter, locked transport; about ₹1-1.5 lakh [A] (basis: not priced; get 3 quotes). Partners: at least 3 BIS-licensed refiners [S52] and one independent assay lab. We burn nothing ourselves, so we avoid running an incinerator [I].

**Why an incumbent would not copy it quickly.**
- A refiner can promise transparency, and some abroad market "transparent payouts" [S32]. But a refiner cannot be a neutral judge of its own purchase, and an auction makes refiners compete on payable share [I].
- Large Indian refiners built collection networks for consumer scrap, not workshop sweeps (MMTC-PAMP collection centres [S46]). Tiny lots are uneconomic for them unless pooled [I].
- Collectors earn from the information gap, so the model attacks their margin [I].
- The moat is thin: trust and density in one cluster, plus lot-history data. A refiner group could copy within 12-18 months [A] (basis: no technical barrier; test by asking 3 refiners whether they would join or compete).

**What must be true.**
1. Small owners realise 75% or less of the contained gold today [A] (basis: below this, a 4% fee still leaves the seller about 9 points better off; test: the assay comparison below).
2. At least 3 licensed refiners will bid on pooled small lots and sign a witness and umpire clause.
3. Owners will move waste sales from cash to invoiced sales with 3% GST [S36].
4. A witness-only operation needs no pollution consent of its own (assay centres do need one [S35]; we would not burn or assay).

**Biggest risk.** Informality. If owners or karigars sell waste for cash off the books, a paper trail is a cost to them, not a benefit [A] (basis: no source checked; test by asking owners how they were paid for their last lot).

**Cheapest test (under ₹50,000).** In one cluster, take split samples of 10 current or recent waste lots, get independent fire assays, and compare contained value with what each owner was paid last time. Success: median realisation of 75% or less. Cost: ₹25,000-45,000 for assays, travel and seals [A] (basis: no Indian sweeps assay price found; BIS centres charge ₹45 per article with a ₹200 minimum for consumer tests [S33] and ₹75 per article for hallmarking from 2026 [S34], but neither is a sweeps price; get 2 quotes).

**Prior-art check (6 searches).** Queries: "jewellery workshop waste sweeps collection aggregator India startup gold recovery transparent assay"; "jewelers sweeps lot witnessed melt split sample umpire assay independent settlement refinery dispute"; "online auction platform precious metal scrap sweeps refiners bid sellers marketplace gold bearing waste"; "patents.google.com method sampling assaying precious metal sweeps jewelry waste lot homogenization settlement"; "study informal gold recovery jewellery workshop waste India mercury scavengers recovery efficiency urban mining paper"; "Augmont OR MMTC-PAMP OR Kundan Refinery jewellery manufacturing waste sweeps polishing dust recovery service India jewellers".
- Closest things: refiner umpire procedures [S23]; a refiner marketing transparent sweeps payouts abroad [S32]; general online scrap auctions (ScrapBays [S27], MSA ScrapSite with sealed bids [S28]); a US trademark for an online market for precious-metal refining materials, status unknown [S26]; Kolkata collectors who pay for sweeping rights [S12].
- Patents and standards cover sampling and homogenising, not the market: ISO 11596:2021 [S24], US8399529 [S25].
- Papers: lab studies recover about 92% of gold from workshop floor sweepings [S29]; a thesis says small workshops send waste to processors and recover poorly [S30]. No study of Indian informal recovery yields found.
- Real difference: a neutral witness plus split assay plus competing refiner terms, for small Indian workshop lots. No close match for that combination found in 6 searches. Crowdfunding: not searched separately for this concept.

**Confidence.** Medium that a realisation gap exists (several sources point the same way, but each figure is weak). Low that owners will formalise waste sales.

### Lead 2: Capture lease paid in gold

**Method.** Business model shift, borrowed from energy performance contracting: the supplier funds the asset and is paid from measured savings [K]. Mechanism transfer from photo labs, where recovery firms placed exchange cartridges on fixer lines, swapped them, and paid for the silver [K]; a 1982-era patent covers such an exchange cartridge [S19].

**Core mechanism.** Fund capture only on streams the plant does not capture today: hand-wash and rinse water, polishing air through a water curtain, mop water. Every new stream ends in a sealed, numbered cartridge or bag that is opened only at the refiner, with the client watching. Client and firm split the fine gold from those new streams for a fixed term.

**How it works.**
1. Trap test (2-4 weeks): fit a hand-wash trap (listed at ₹20,000 [S15]) on one basin and a settling trap on one rinse line. Seal, collect, and assay through the Lead 1 process.
2. Contract signed before full install: 24-month term. Kit chosen by plant: settling tank (listed at ₹50,000 [S15]); hand-wash station (₹1,35,000-2,07,000 [S16]); water-curtain cabinet for polishing motors (a common design [S22]).
3. New-stream rule: we share only in gold from streams with no capture before install, recorded with photos. Existing bench skins and bag filters stay fully the client's. This avoids baseline arguments [I].
4. Monthly: swap sealed cartridges; refine through a licensed refiner; split fine gold 25% to us, 75% to the client [A] (basis: leaves most value with the client; test in negotiation).
5. Optional: a small share of new-stream gold goes to the floor team, to reduce sabotage [A].
6. Month 25: the client owns the kit; we offer processing for a fee.

**What the buyer experiences.** No capital outlay. No change at the bench. A monthly statement in grams, with sealed bags they can audit.

**Measurable advantage against the best current alternative** (buy capture equipment outright: a US wash-water system at USD 6,610, about ₹5.8 lakh [A] [S17]; Indian tanks and stations at ₹20,000-2,70,000 [S15, S16]).
- Zero cash outlay and no payback risk; the client pays only in grams it was losing [I].
- For a 50-worker plant at 12.5 kg a month [A], new capture of 0.1-0.3% of throughput [A] is 12.5-37.5 g a month, worth ₹1,78,750-5,36,250 [I]. The client keeps 75%: ₹1.34-4.02 lakh a month [I].
- Basis for 0.1-0.3%: the listed manufacturer cut net loss by about 1 point with ERP, filtration and process change [S5]; a filtration vendor claims up to an extra 20 g per kg processed, which is 2% [S18] (vendor claim; I use 5-15% of it); a large Indian factory in the 1990s lost about 10 kg per tonne, 1%, on floors [S10]. Against this, Mumbai jewellers had already begun vacuuming dust and collecting wash water by 2010 [S47], so some plants capture already.

**Who pays, price logic, frequency.** The client pays monthly, in fine gold, 25% of new-stream recovery for 24 months [A]. Revenue per 50-worker site: ₹44,700-1,34,000 a month [I]. A kit of ₹1.3-2.2 lakh [A] (basis: listed prices [S15, S16] plus a water-curtain unit, not priced) pays back in 1-5 months [I]. The ₹5,00,000 capital funds 2-3 kits [I].

**Production or delivery outline.** Buy tanks and stations from Indian makers [S15, S16]; source water-curtain cabinets locally; tamper-evident seals; refine through licensed refiners [S52]. Founder installs and swaps; one technician from site 4.

**Why an incumbent would not copy it quickly.**
- Equipment makers sell for cash; they do not refine, carry assets on their balance sheet, or settle in gold [I].
- Refiners abroad chose free audits to win refining business [S54], not funded equipment; I found no refiner kit-for-waste programme in 3 searches. A refiner could still copy this. That is the main competitive risk [I].
- Defences: the new-stream protocol; sealed custody that also protects the client from internal dust theft [S38, S39]; per-site yield data that prices the next contract.

**What must be true.**
1. Plants with 30-200 workers lose at least 0.1% of throughput through uncaptured streams [A] (basis: low case above; test: the trap test below).
2. They accept an outsider's sealed bins on their floor.
3. They sign before they see the full yield, or accept a test fee if they decline.

**Biggest risk.** The client copies the hardware. A ₹50,000 tank [S15] is cheap; once a trap test shows gold, the plant can buy its own [I]. The service must be worth more than the box: custody, processing and settlement. Mitigation: a signed pilot letter. If the yield passes an agreed level, the client signs the 24-month contract or pays a test fee of ₹15,000 [A] (basis: covers test cost; test in the first 3 pitches).

**Cheapest test (under ₹50,000).** Three plants, one hand-wash basin and one rinse line each, for 4 weeks; witnessed refining and assay. Success: at least 0.05% of throughput recovered from those two streams alone [A] (basis: a third of the low case). Cost: ₹30,000-50,000 [A] (basis: two traps at listed prices [S15] reused across plants, plus assays and travel).

**Prior-art check (6 searches).** Queries: "refiner installs free filtration dust collection system jewelry manufacturers in exchange for refining waste program"; "shared savings OR gain share precious metal recovery service jewelry manufacturers outcome based"; "gold recovery system jewellery manufacturers no upfront cost pay from recovered gold rental lease filtration India OR worldwide"; "patent jewelry polishing wastewater precious metal particle recovery filter cartridge tamper sealed exchange"; "kickstarter OR indiegogo jewelry gold dust recovery polishing dust collector bench sweep"; "gold content jewellery polishing sludge wastewater characterization recovery study grams per kg throughput".
- Closest products: Romanoff wash-water system, sold outright [S17]; Filternox filtration for gold manufacturers [S18].
- Patents: a tank with 100 and 20 micron filters for jewellery wash water (1993) [S20]; an exchange cartridge for precious metal in solution [S19]. Both are old and probably expired; neither covers the contract.
- Papers: gravity separation recovered at most 68% from real hand-wash waste (2000 study) [S31].
- Crowdfunding: none found in 1 search.
- Pay-from-recovered-gold lease for jewellery capture: no close match found in 3 searches (the first three queries above).

**Confidence.** Medium that uncaptured streams hold gold worth the effort in plants without filtration. Low that the contract survives the client-copy problem.

### Lead 3: Weight-guaranteed finishing hub

**Method.** Consolidation and elimination: the workshop removes its own polishing step. Inversion: the finisher credits the customer for removed metal instead of keeping it.

**Core mechanism.** Polishing scatters gold into air, clothes and drains [S39]. Move that one step to a sealed hub with full wet capture. Weigh every lot in and out to 0.001 g, and credit the customer's metal account for a fixed share of the weight removed.

**How it works.**
1. The customer brings filed and assembled pieces. The lot is weighed, photographed and put in a sealed tray.
2. Pieces are polished in enclosed water-curtain cabinets, the wet-collection principle of a 1978-era US patent for buffing hoods [S21], in current designs [S22].
3. The lot is weighed out. The receipt shows weight removed.
4. The customer's account is credited with 70% of removed weight, adjusted for karat, in fine gold, settled monthly or set against fees [A] (basis: leaves a spread; test in the pilot). If the hub captures about 95% [A] and refines at about 92% payable [S8], it keeps about 17% of removed metal [I] (0.95 × 0.92 − 0.70).
5. A fee per gram covers labour.

**What the buyer experiences.** No motor, no dust, no polisher to watch, and a receipt in milligrams.

**Measurable advantage against the best current alternative.**
- In-house polishing without enclosed collection: one software vendor puts filing and polishing loss at 1.5-3% of throughput (vendor claim, no method given) [S49].
- Outside polishers raise trust problems: a Bengaluru polisher took 16 g from a 50 g chain [S40]; a polishing firm's dust account was under-recorded by more than half [S38].
- For a workshop sending 2 kg a month with 0.5% removed in polishing [A], 10 g is removed; a 70% credit returns 7 g, worth ₹1,00,100 a month [I], against nothing returned if the dust is uncaptured or kept by the polisher [A] (basis: no source on who keeps dust in Indian polishing job work; test in interviews).

**Who pays, price logic, frequency.** The customer pays a per-gram fee of ₹10-25 [A] (basis: none found after 3 searches; test by asking 10 workshops what they pay polishers today) plus the hub's spread on recovered metal. Daily use. At 20 kg a month through the hub: fees ₹2-5 lakh, plus a spread of 10-35 g worth ₹1.5-5 lakh a month [I] (removal of 0.3-1% assumed [A]).

**Production or delivery outline.** Rent 300-500 square feet in a zone where equipment is allowed, near a cluster. Two enclosed stations, 2-3 polishers, safe, CCTV, insurance for gold in custody. Capex ₹2.5-4 lakh [A] (basis: a polishing machine with dust collector is listed at ₹4,72,000 including GST, Hunter's source [S60]; basic motors cost far less; get quotes). A van variant (concept 8) can follow for markets where equipment is not allowed [S6].

**Why an incumbent would not copy it quickly.**
- Polishers who keep the dust would give up a hidden margin by offering a credit [A] (basis: not verified; test in interviews).
- GJEPC common facility centres offer machines at a reasonable or nominal fee [S41, S42], but as machine access, not as a weight-guaranteed service. A CFC that adds capture and credit is the closest competitor [I].
- Equipment makers sell machines and do not run services [I].

**What must be true.**
1. Workshops already outsource polishing or will. Polishing firms exist in Bengaluru [S38, S40], and polishing is a defined job role in the national skills framework [S61], but how common outsourcing is in Kolkata, Rajkot or Mumbai was not found.
2. Same-day turnaround.
3. An insurer will cover gold in custody at a bearable premium.
4. A 70% credit beats what workshops recover today [A] (basis: no source on current polishing recovery; test in the 15 interviews).

**Biggest risk.** Custody. Holding 1-2 kg of customers' gold means ₹1.43-2.86 crore on the premises [I] against ₹5,00,000 of capital. One theft or one dispute ends the firm.

**Cheapest test (under ₹50,000).** First, interview 15 workshop owners on who polishes, what they pay, and who keeps the dust. Then a 2-week pilot: rent time on an existing polisher's or CFC's machine, add a portable wet-capture unit, run 5 customers' lots with weigh-in and weigh-out receipts. Cost: ₹30,000-45,000 [A] (basis: not priced; get quotes).

## 6. Wildcard: Karigar gold account

**Method.** Inversion of who owns bench waste.

**Mechanism.**
- Each karigar gets a personal sealed waste kit: bench skin, apron and hand-wash pouch.
- Each month the kit is refined and assayed using the Lead 1 process.
- A share, say 20-30% of the fine gold recovered, is credited to the karigar's own gold account [A] (basis: large enough to change behaviour; test with 2 workshops). The owner gets the rest at refinery payout instead of losing it to sweepers or skimming.
- The account follows the karigar between workshops and doubles as a verified loss record.
- We charge 5% of recovered gold [A].

**Why it might work.**
- Karigars are scarce and non-exclusive [S51]. A workshop that offers a gold account has a hiring advantage [I].
- It turns the people who control capture into its beneficiaries. Hardware cannot do that [I].
- It reuses Lead 1's settlement rails, so the extra cost is mostly software and kits [I].

**Why it is risky.**
- Owners may not want karigars to see what waste is worth.
- Karigars might push finished metal into their bins. A daily mass balance is needed to stop this.
- Collectors and karigar side-income lose out, so expect resistance [A] (basis: blockers named in [S10, S12]; test in interviews).
- Prior art: not searched (budget). I make no novelty claim.
