## 3. Buying process and trigger

**Regulatory frame (the deciding question).**
- I could not read the Indian text. Commentary says supplier audits are part of the revised Schedule M quality system [S2][S3].
- In 2023 India considered a rule making drugmakers audit raw-material suppliers at least once a year [S1]. The same report said this annual audit would replace routine supplier audits [S1]. I found no final notification after 2 searches.
- Guidance that interprets the PIC/S GMP guide says manufacturers must audit their highest-risk (Category 1) suppliers. It allows an on-site audit to be skipped if there is objective evidence of a recent audit by a reputable organisation. If a report comes through a third party, the manufacturer must take reasonable steps to check it is valid. Lowest-risk (Category 3) suppliers need no audit [S4].
- TGA guidance says the supplier should normally be the actual maker of the material, not a broker or agent [S5].
- The EU accepts third-party and joint audits if EU GMP Chapter 7 contracts are in place. The Qualified Person remains responsible for evaluating the report [S6][S7]. One shared-audit vendor says reports sold to many buyers now face more regulatory scrutiny of auditor independence [S8].
- IPEC says the excipient user must know the full supply chain of each batch. It must audit the maker and each distributor, or rely on an audit someone else did [S9]. The paper is also hosted by IPEC India, an industry body registered in 2014 [S10]. A distributor's CoA should name the original maker and site [S54].
- A RAPS item reports a proposal to let accredited private auditors issue compliance certificates in India. It says regulators keep the right to inspect anyway. I could not confirm its status or date [S53].
- Inference: a shared report is most defensible if the plant's own QA reviews it, signs it off under its own vendor-qualification SOP, and the report covers the actual manufacturer, not only the trader [I] (from [S4][S5][S6][S9]). Whether Indian state inspectors accept this is untested. Confidence low.

**Likely buying journey** (all [A] unless tagged; to be checked in interviews).
1. Trigger: an inspection observation, a notice, or a CDSCO directive on excipients [S48][S60].
2. The QA head checks what the plant's consultant or SOP template already provides. Vendor-qualification SOP templates use a questionnaire, document review, and on-site audits only for critical suppliers [S56] [A] (assumed to be typical in small plants; test with QA heads).
3. The QA head asks the owner for spend. The owner compares the cost with a consultant visit, a free supplier questionnaire, and testing.
4. Veto points: the owner (cost); the trader (refuses access or hides the source); the consultant (sees a rival); the inspector (rejects the report).
5. Time: unknown. Likely fast just before or after an inspection, slow otherwise [A].

## 6. Willingness-to-pay signals

Positive:
- Large Indian excipient makers have paid for third-party EXCiPACT certification since about 2015 to reduce customer audits [S12][S13][S15]. This shows that suppliers pay. It does not show that small buyers pay [I].
- Eurofins Assurance India became an EXCiPACT certifying body for 2025-2028 [S11]. A certifier would take this step only if it expected Indian demand [I].
- Qualifyze is building a 7-person India supplier team [S28] and hiring for India sales [S29]. This signals money spent on Indian supplier audits, probably mostly by export-facing buyers [I] (based on its global client base, [S30]).
- A third-party auditor worked on site in Baddi in October 2025, for a large firm [S23].

Negative or absent:
- No evidence that micro or small domestic plants pay for supplier audits or reports (C4).
- Price anchors offered to small plants are low: Rs 3,500 per vendor audit [S18] and GMP packages from Rs 24,999 [S34].
- Small units are short of cash. Only about 1,700 of 6,500 MSMEs (26%) filed upgrade plans by the deadline [S37]. A Gujarat association warned 4,000-5,000 units could close [S41]. Closures were reported in Himachal [S39][S40].

Overall: willingness to pay is shown for suppliers and large buyers. It is not shown for the target buyer [I].

## 8. Reasons buyers would not switch

1. Regulatory doubt. Inspectors' acceptance in India is unknown (C2). A QA head who is unsure will not risk a finding [A].
2. A cheap status quo. Questionnaire, CoA and lot testing cost little in cash [S56] [A].
3. Testing is now the regulator's focus. The 2025 directive tells states to make plants test raw materials [S48]. Plants may spend on testing rather than audits [I].
4. The regulator now verifies the riskiest solvent makers. ONDLS licensing and batch upload cover propylene glycol, glycerin, sorbitol, maltitol, PEG under 1,000 and others [S42][S43]. That covers part of what a shared audit would check [I]. Enforcement has begun: a solvent maker was accused of making pharma-grade propylene glycol without a licence [S44].
5. Auditing the trader may not be enough. TGA says the supplier should normally be the actual maker [S5]. IPEC says the user must know the whole chain [S9]. A report on a trader only is weak; a report on the maker may already exist (EXCiPACT, report libraries) [I].
6. Traders may refuse. Some suppliers hide the original manufacturer [S55]. A trader protects its margin by hiding its source [A] (common trading behaviour; test with 5 traders).
7. Cash squeeze after upgrades. Units that survived upgrades are short of cash, and many closed [S37][S39][S41].
8. The plant's consultant may already include vendor audits, or may see a rival [S19][S21] [A].
9. Liability and trust. If an audited supplier later ships bad material, the plant is still responsible [S6] [A] (EU principle; Indian position not checked).

## 9. Demand verdict

**Weak L1. Confidence medium on the level; low on the size.**

- The task is compulsory and enforced. Supplier approval is part of revised Schedule M [S2][S3]. Inspections are active [S60][S36].
- People elsewhere pay for shared reports and certificates. Large Indian suppliers pay for EXCiPACT [S12][S15]. Global buyers pay Qualifyze and Eurofins [S28][S23].
- No source shows small domestic plants paying for supplier audits (C4). Indian acceptance of shared reports is unknown (C2).
- The space is not empty. Consultants, report libraries, certifiers, and now a regulator database all touch this need [S18][S20][S11][S42].
- Hunt B is partly right: this is close to consulting and crowded at the top. Hunt C is partly right: no shared, low-price product for micro plants and their traders was found [I].
- Biggest risk: the riskiest inputs (solvents bought through traders) are where small plants are least willing to pay, and the regulator is covering them through licensing and testing [I] (from [S42][S48][S46]).

## 10. Cheapest credible demand test

The founder runs this; I contact no one. Three gated steps. Stop at the first failure.

**Step 1: regulatory check (1-2 weeks; about Rs 6,000 [I]).**
- Who: the Himachal State Drugs Controller's office (Baddi) and 2 working or retired drug inspectors or senior QA consultants in Baddi or Ahmedabad. Optionally file an RTI with CDSCO asking for any guidance on third-party supplier audit reports.
- Ask: "Would a third-party audit report on a supplier's actual manufacturing site, reviewed and approved under the plant's own vendor-qualification SOP, satisfy the supplier-approval requirement of revised Schedule M?"
- Success: at least 2 of 3 say it is acceptable with the plant's own review. Failure: any official says the plant must do its own audit.
- Cost: 8 hours at Rs 750 = Rs 6,000 [I]; RTI fee assumed Rs 10 [A] (verify).

**Step 2: problem and spend interviews (2 weeks; about Rs 22,500 [I]).**
- Who: 20 QA heads or owners of small plants in Baddi-Barotiwala-Nalagarh and Gujarat. Find them through the state licence lists, IndiaMART manufacturer listings, and the Himachal and Gujarat drug manufacturers' associations.
- Ask about past behaviour, not opinions: which excipient suppliers they use, which are traders, what they did for vendor qualification in the last 12 months, what they spent, and whether an inspector raised vendor qualification.
- Success: at least 8 of 20 had a vendor-qualification observation or spent money on supplier audits in the last 12 months, and at least 5 name the same 2-3 suppliers. Failure: fewer than 4 report any spend or observation.
- Cost: 30 hours at Rs 750 = Rs 22,500 [I]; calls only, no travel.

**Step 3: pre-sale with deposits (3-4 weeks; about Rs 55,000 [I]).**
- First get written consent from one commonly used supplier (ideally the maker of a high-risk excipient, or a trader that will disclose its maker) to be audited and to let the report be shared.
- Then offer the report to 30 plants that use that supplier at Rs 15,000 plus GST, with a refundable deposit of Rs 5,000 [A] (price to test). The deposit is refunded if fewer than 4 plants commit.
- Success (L3 to L4): supplier consent plus at least 5 paid deposits from 30 offers. Failure: supplier refuses sharing, or fewer than 3 deposits.
- Cost: 40 hours at Rs 750 = Rs 30,000 [I], plus one Baddi trip of about Rs 25,000 [A] (travel and stay estimate). Do not book the audit itself (Rs 45,000-60,000 [A]) until deposits cover it.

**Total if all three steps run:** about Rs 83,500 and 7-8 weeks [I] (Rs 6,000 + Rs 22,500 + Rs 55,000). That is about 17% of founder capital [I] (Rs 83,500 / Rs 5,00,000), and most of it is founder time, not cash [I].
