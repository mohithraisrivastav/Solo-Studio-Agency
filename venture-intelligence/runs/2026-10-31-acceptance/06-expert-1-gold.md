### 0.1 How gold-bearing waste is sampled and assayed (applies to every concept)

**What practice says.**
- Refiners first burn sweeps at low heat to remove combustible material [S2]. Practitioners then grind the ash, pull out magnetic iron, and melt with a soda ash, nitre and borax flux before assay [S1].
- The lot is screened. Fine powder is sampled. Oversize pieces, mostly karat metal, are melted with copper and assayed as a bullion sample [S1]. This is the screen-metallics method. Mining labs choose screen fire assay over standard fire assay when coarse gold is present, because coarse grains bias small aliquots [S3]. A 2026 study reports single coarse grains causing several-fold swings in measured gold content [S4].
- Small lots are often not assayed at all. Forum practitioners say lots refined in aqua regia are settled on out-turn (gold actually recovered), and larger lots are fire assayed [S1]. Forum evidence only; confidence medium-low.
- A commercial refiner melts clean scrap with flux, takes a pin sample, and fire assays it in duplicate [S2].
- Umpire practice is standard abroad. If the seller's and refiner's assays fall within an agreed splitting limit, the average settles [S10, S11]. If not, a sealed umpire sample goes to a third-party lab [S11]; one refiner requires this within 4 business days [S10]. The party whose assay is farther from the umpire's usually pays the umpire fee [S11]. One guideline gives the customer 15 days to dispute [S12]. One brochure describes a three-way sealed split: refiner, customer and a sealed dispute sample [S13]. Splitting limits are set per contract; I found no typical value for sweeps (not found in 1 search: "splitting limit precious metal settlement assay exchange umpire laboratory loser pays sweeps refining customer sample tolerance gold").

**Cupellation is the wrong test for raw sweeps.**
- ISO 11426 (cupellation) covers gold content preferably between 100 and 999.5 parts per thousand [S7]. IS 1418 covers bullion, alloys and jewellery by cupellation [S8].
- Sweeps at 1-8% gold are 10-80 parts per thousand [I] (from the content table in 0.2), below that range.
- So the Inventor's idea of sending raw waste splits to BIS hallmarking centres does not work directly [I]. Waste must either (a) be assayed by crucible (lead-collection) fire assay at an ore-type lab, or (b) be melted to a bullion button first, with the button cupelled and the slag assayed separately [I].

**XRF.**
- XRF reads the surface only and is affected by plating and coatings [S6]. Vendors report results within about one-eighth of a karat of fire assay on solid jewellery [S5]; that is a vendor figure on solid alloy.
- No data found supporting XRF on mixed sweeps powder (not found in 1 search: "XRF gold analysis limitations surface only plated heterogeneous powder sweeps not suitable fire assay comparison accuracy"). XRF is a screening tool; fire assay settles disputes [S6]. For these concepts, XRF is useful only on melted buttons or pins, never on raw waste [I].

**Indian assay price.** The Indian Bureau of Mines lists gold by fire assay with AAS finish at ₹4,583 per sample, ₹5,408 with tax, and gold plus silver at ₹6,112, ₹7,212 with tax, from 1 April 2026 [S9]. The column headings were not visible in the summary; I read the second figure as including 18% GST [I] (₹4,583 × 1.18 = ₹5,408). Sample preparation is a separate charge [S9]. No private Indian price for a sweeps assay was found. Bangalore Refinery says it recovers from manufacturing wastage and tests by fire assay and XRF [S14]; its terms were not found.

**How representative a sample can be (Calculation C1).**
- Method: count the gold particles of the largest size that fit in one assay aliquot. Relative standard deviation is about 1/√N (Poisson counting) [K]. This is a simplified form of Gy's fundamental sampling error, which falls as sample mass rises and as particle size falls [S3].
- Inputs: alloy density 15.6 g/cm³ for 18K-22K [K]; grade 1% and 5% [A] (basis: sweeps range in 0.2; test: assay real lots); aliquot 30 g and 50 g [K] (common fire-assay charge sizes).
- Results: at 1% grade in a 30 g aliquot, top particles of 0.15 mm give about 1.0% relative error; 0.5 mm give 5.8%; 1 mm give 16.5% [I]. At 5% grade, 0.5 mm gives 2.6% and 1 mm gives 7.4% [I].
- Reading: this is a worst case (all gold at top size). If particles above about 0.2 mm are screened out and melted whole, a 30-50 g aliquot of fines can represent the lot to about 1% [I]. Gold is malleable and flattens in a mill instead of breaking, so screening, not more grinding, is the fix [K].
- For lots under about 1-2 kg of ash, melting the whole lot to a button plus slag avoids most sampling error [I]. Molten metal is homogeneous [K]; the remaining risk is gold left in the slag, which needs its own assay [I].
- Homogenising wet sludge for sampling is itself patented prior art (US8399529) [S89], which shows it is hard [I].
- Weighing at pickup proves custody, not gold. Sludge and sweeps carry water and organics, and burning removes them [S2]. A wet weight at the workshop cannot be the price basis [I].

### 0.3 Rules that apply to anyone handling gold waste in India

- **Pollution consent.** Under CPCB's 2016 scheme, a gold and silver smithy doing acid purification is Red if it uses more than 1 litre of sulphuric or nitric acid a month, and Green at or below that [S26, S27]. Maharashtra lists gold smithy among activities with no process effluent [S28]. One secondary source lists jewellery and goldsmith workshops as White [S67]; Maharashtra grants White consents by self-certification [S68]. State lists differ [S67]. Confidence: medium; confirm with the state board.
- **Assay labs.** CPCB's October 2020 guidelines require assaying and hallmarking centres to hold Water Act and Air Act consents; NGT told state boards to enforce them in January 2021 and again in September 2023 [S31, S33]. Cupellation and parting emit lead oxides and nitrous fumes [S31]. A BIS document says these centres were put in the Orange category and many in crowded markets struggle to get consent [S32].
- **Hazardous waste.** Processing, recycling and recovery need authorisation under Rule 6 of the Hazardous and Other Wastes Rules 2016 [S29]. A Maharashtra board record shows a unit seeking Rule 9 approval to recover gold and silver from ETP sludge and crucible dust [S30]. Whether workshop sweeps are a listed hazardous waste was not confirmed (1 search: "Hazardous and Other Wastes Rules 2016 precious metal recovery jewellery waste authorisation Schedule IV recycling gold sludge"). An environmental consultant must confirm.
- **BIS.** The BIS refinery licence covers 999 and 995 bullion and coin, needs in-house manufacturing and testing, and is granted for one year [S34]. DGFT lets only BIS-licensed refineries import doré (sources say from 2017 or 2018) [S35]. I found no rule requiring a BIS licence to refine a client's scrap [I] (absence after 1 search is not proof). Hallmarking has been mandatory for gold jewellery and artefacts since 16 June 2021 [S83]; waste is not hallmarked [I].
- **GST.** Sweepings containing gold (HSN 7112 99 20) carry 3% GST in most sources; one outlier says 0% [S39]. Chapter 71 goods are exempt from e-way bills under Rule 138(14) per GSTN's 6 February 2025 advisory [S36]; Kerala requires them for intra-state movement [S38]; one June 2026 secondary source claims inter-state e-way bills are now mandatory [S37]. I trust the GSTN advisory more until a notification is seen (confidence medium). Job work on jewellery is 5% in most sources [S40] and 18% in one [S41]. Gold a job worker retains beyond its allowance was ruled consideration taxed at 5% [S42]. Goods sent for job work must come back within one year (Section 143) [S43].
- **PMLA.** Dealers in precious metals are reporting entities for cash transactions of ₹10 lakh or more with one customer, single or linked (G.S.R. 799(E), 28 December 2020) [S44]. KYC thresholds conflict between sources [S44].
- **Labour.** Under the OSH Code a factory has 20 or more workers with power or 40 without; establishments with 10 or more workers are covered [S65]. The Code applies from 21 November 2025 [S82].
- **Insurance.** Jewellers' block policies cover manufacturers and job workers, and goods held by goldsmiths and job workers [S46]. Section I is compulsory; premises are rated Class I to III by security [S47]. One insurer lists its transit section at 0.10% of the sum insured [S45]. Employee or karigar dishonesty was the largest claims category for one broker in FY 2025-26 [S48]. Premises rates were not found. A consumer article gives 0.3-1% a year for jewellery cover; that is not a trade rate [S90].
- **Secure transport.** Sequel's 2026 exhibition tariff charges by gross weight with a 10 kg minimum chargeable weight per consignment, 0.05% enhanced liability if Sequel insures, plus 18% GST [S49]. Intra-city rates not found (1 search: "secure logistics gold jewellery transport India charges per consignment Sequel BVC Brinks jewellers intra-city valuables courier rates").

### 10. Cheapest test to retire the biggest technical risk

### 2. Findings by discipline (tagged)

**Separation physics.**
- In still water, gold-alloy particles of 10 µm fall 1 m in about 21 minutes; 2 µm particles take nearly 9 hours [I] (C3, Stokes law [K]).
- So settling catches coarse particles fast, but fines need long still residence or a filter [I]. Patents note settling tanks need a long undisturbed residence time and add traps for fines [S55, S56]. Stagnant tanks can smell [S55].
- Ultrasonic cleaning fluids often stop settling tanks working [S57].
- On a real hand-wash sample, gravity recovery reached at most 68%, against 90% in theory for particles above 35 µm [S17].
- Soap and polishing compound may hold fines in suspension or float them [I] (basis: compound residues are a known topic [S22]; no measured effect found in 1 search: "jewelry hand wash water gold recovery settling tank efficiency fine particles soap polishing compound float filter micron percentage captured").
- A vendor claims manufacturers recover at least 90% of precious metals in wastewater [S57]; that is marketing.

**Baseline: many "uncaptured" streams are already sold.**
- A Delhi goldsmith said even leftover wash water is saleable, and a contractor buys both dust sacks and water (2018) [S58].
- Mumbai accounts describe collecting hand-wash and shower water and selling it in bulk [S87]; Mumbai jewellers had begun vacuuming dust and collecting wash water by 2010 [S87].
- Reading: the Inventor's "new-stream rule" assumes zero capture before install. Where a collector already pays for that water or dust, the stream is not new, and the client loses the collector's payment [I]. Baseline disputes are likely [I]. Confidence: medium (two news sources, two cities).

## Lead 3: Weight-guaranteed finishing hub

### 1. Disciplines and deciding questions
- **Process engineering.** How much metal does polishing remove, and what share can a hub capture?
- **Metrology.** Can weight in minus weight out be trusted as "metal removed"?
- **Finance and custody.** How much customer gold sits in the hub, and who funds the credits until refining pays?
- **Safety and security.** Dust, fire and theft.
- **Regulation.** Category of a polishing unit, job-work papers, GST on the spread.

### 2. Findings by discipline (tagged)

**Removal and capture.**
- No authoritative figure for polishing removal was found. One article shows a 1.0% polishing allowance as a hypothetical example only [S25]. A software vendor claims 1.5-3% loss for filing and polishing without enclosed collection (reported in 05, not re-checked) [S85]. - Polishing machines with dust collection are sold in India: ₹4,72,000 including GST [S52]. Basic dust collectors cost ₹18,000-21,000 [S50, S51].
- Recovery from polishing dust can be very high: direct melting recovered 99.97% in one test [S18]. Capture at the machine is the weak link, not refining [I].

**Metrology.** Weight in minus weight out mixes metal removed with compound residue left on the piece, lost stones and moisture [I]. Compound residue in gold alloys is a known issue [S22]. A balance of 0.001 g is precise enough; the error is in what the weight change means [I].

**Karat.** Removed metal is alloy. 10 g of 22K removed is 9.16 g of fine gold; 10 g of 18K is 7.5 g [I] (C5).

**Spread and break-even.** With a 70% credit and 92% payable [S74], the hub breaks even at 76% capture [I]. At 80% capture its spread is 3.6% of removed weight; at 95%, 17.4% [I] (C5). The Inventor's 17% spread assumes 95% capture [A] (Inventor's assumption), which is unproven.

**Custody and working capital.**
- At 20 kg a month and one day in custody, the hub holds 800 g, ₹1.13 crore; at three days, ₹3.39 crore [I] (C5).
- Credits owed at 70% of removal: ₹5.9 lakh a month at 0.3% removal, ₹9.9 lakh at 0.5%, ₹19.8 lakh at 1% [I] (C5).
- Refining abroad takes 4-6 weeks [S73]. A two-month gap between crediting customers and being paid by the refiner needs ₹11.9-39.5 lakh of gold working capital [I] (C5). That breaks the ₹5 lakh constraint unless credits are paid only after the refiner pays [I].

**Incentive problem.** The hub earns more when it removes more metal, while customers sell jewellery by weight [I]. Over-polishing is a hidden cost to the customer. Per-design removal limits are needed [I].

**Insurance.** At 0.3-1% a year [S90] (consumer rate band, not a trade rate), cover on ₹1.13-3.39 crore costs ₹33,900-3,39,000 a year [I] (C5). Real premiums not found (2 searches). Employee dishonesty is the largest claims category for one broker [S48], so the policy's infidelity cover matters [I].

**Regulation.** Polishing without acid is likely White or Green [S27, S67]; acid pickling above 1 litre a month makes it Red [S26]. Under 10 workers the OSH Code's establishment rules do not bite; under 20 with power it is not a factory [S65]. Customers send goods under a job-work challan and must get them back within a year [S43]. Polishing job work is 5% GST in most sources [S40]. The gold the hub keeps (its spread) is consideration taxed as part of the job-work fee [S42]. Holding and crediting customers' fine gold may make the hub a dealer for PMLA [S44] [I].

**Demand-side check.** No Indian polishing-only rate was found; making charges bundle polishing into ₹100-250 per gram in older Rajkot practice [S71]. GJEPC's Rajkot CFC lists laser, 3D and refining machines; usage fees not found [S70].

- A labour lawyer: whether a recovery share is wages, and the payment mode.
- A financial-regulation lawyer: deposits law, Companies Act, gold products.
- A chartered accountant: GST and TDS on bonuses and fees.
