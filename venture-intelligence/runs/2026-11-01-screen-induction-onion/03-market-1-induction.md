# 03 Market screen 1: battery-buffered induction station (thesis T1)

Date: 2026-11-01. Investigator: Buyer and Market Investigator. About 30 searches used. WebFetch could not open any site (merc.gov.in, mahadiscom.in, nobroker.in, dcciapune.org and others did not resolve in this session), so every source below is `search summary` only. No official MERC or MSEDCL document was opened. Treat all tariff numbers as unverified.

## Verdict (short)

Reject. The Rs 3.5 lakh saving reproduces arithmetically [I] but only if MSEDCL lets an eatery that physically draws more than 20 kW stay in the up-to-20 kW tariff band. I found no rule that says so, and the cheaper rules I did find point the other way. Without that, the battery earns nothing and the buyer loses the whole cost. Even if it works, a plain LPG-assist or load-staggering route gets much of the same saving without a battery. Full verdict in the last section.

Price is now a weaker trigger: commercial LPG rose again on 1 Oct 2026 (Mumbai Rs 2,764.50) but is far below the May peak above Rs 3,000 [S10, S9].

- Blockers named in news: strong connection needed [S25], cooking is slower on induction [S22], landlord and building capacity [S25]. Nobody I found mentions a battery as a wanted fix.

## 8. Reasons buyers would not switch

- Induction is slower and some cooks and menus (dosa, tandoor, deep frying) resist [S22, S21].
- Piped gas, where available, is cheaper than LPG and needs no electrical work [S23].
- Landlord, building electrical capacity, and fire concerns about lithium in a hot greasy kitchen. No Indian rule on kitchens found; CEA battery safety chapter was only a draft [S29].
- A small owner will buy a Rs 10,000-60,000 cooker before a Rs 3-7 lakh battery system [I] (from listing prices S16-S17 against the capex below).
- The supply shock eased: commercial LPG fell Rs 183.50 on 1 July 2026 and sits below its May peak [S10].
- Any sensible alternative to a battery: stagger burners, keep two gas burners for peaks, or just apply for 25 kW. Compared below.

## A. Tariff and law

### A1. Actual 2026 LT commercial structure

- Official order exists: MERC MYT order in Case No. 75 of 2025 dated 25 March 2026, listed on the MSEDCL tariff page and attached to a DCCIA circular of 27 March 2026 [S3, S4]. I could not open either. No official table seen.
- Only third-party figures (NoBroker, via search summary, effective 1 April 2026): up to 20 kW billed in kWh at Rs 10.11 with Rs 540 a month fixed; above 20 kW to 50 kW billed in kVAh at Rs 13.92 with Rs 550 per kVA a month; ToD peak surcharge 25% 5 pm to midnight [S2]. A different calculator site shows Rs 10.07 per unit and Rs 525 per kVA for the same band, citing another MERC case [S40]. The two disagree; the hunter used the NoBroker set. Contradiction not resolved.
- A July 2025 press report gives 20 kW users Rs 10.37 per unit and 50 kW users Rs 14.22 [S33]. This is an earlier revision, so the 2026 figure may differ again.
- 2018 MSEDCL circular: demand-based and time-of-day tariffs compulsory for LT-II non-domestic consumers with sanctioned load above 20 kW [S5]. Older, may be superseded.
- Billing demand for LT: a quoted MERC extract (Case 226 of 2022) says higher of 65% of maximum demand recorded 06:00-22:00 and 40% of contract demand [S6]. I could not confirm it applies to LT-II or to the 2026 order. If it does, the demand charge follows recorded draw, which a battery could reduce, but only inside the above-20 kW band.
- Not found: whether the 20 kW boundary is judged on sanctioned load or on recorded demand. This is the deciding fact.

### A2. Does cutting peak draw change the billed charge, sanction or category?

Not found. Findings:
- Tariff category follows sanctioned load; MSEDCL ombudsman cases cite a MERC position that tariff is based on sanctioned load, not connected load, and no penalty applies if excess is claimed only on connected-load basis [S7]. A battery therefore does nothing to the category: it stays whatever was sanctioned.
- If the eatery sanctions 20 kW and runs induction that could draw more, exceeding sanctioned load can be billed as excess; ombudsman cases say a consumer who exceeds sanctioned load is obliged to pay [S7]. MD-based penalties for exceeding contract demand exist [S7].
- No rule, circular or order found on load behind a battery or inverter. A 2026 blog says extra-load charges follow contract demand and connected load; low quality [S34].
- Result: I found no support that a battery changes any billed charge or the category. The hunter's saving needs the discom to treat a 30 kW kitchen as a 20 kW consumer.
- Cheapest written answer (for the user to approve; I contact nobody): a letter or Right-to-Information-style query to the MSEDCL Pune circle executive engineer, plus the same question to a MERC consumer-grievance forum, asking: (1) is LT-II tariff band decided on sanctioned load or on recorded maximum demand; (2) is load connected behind a storage system counted as connected load; (3) what happens if recorded kVA exceeds a 20 kW sanction. Cost about Rs 0-500 and 2-6 weeks [A].

### A3. Net metering, storage, ToD

- Maharashtra ToD concession for 9 am to 5 pm continues for commercial and industrial consumers in FY 2026-27, and revised banking rules push C&I users toward storage [S28]. These concern solar and HT industrial users. Nothing found on LT eateries.
- MERC consultation proposes storage requirements for some renewable projects; one vendor says a battery is mandatory above 100 kW since April 2026, while another report calls it only proposed. Conflict; irrelevant to an eatery without solar [S28].
- Night charging: no night tariff for LT-II found. If ToD applies above 20 kW, the 25% evening surcharge [S2] could favour battery discharge in the evening peak, but that only matters in the above-20 kW band. Not tested.
- Utility-scale battery procurement order was set aside by the tribunal [S28]. Not relevant to behind-the-meter, but shows the rules are unsettled.

### Cheaper comparison (no battery)

Keep the sanction at 20 kW and cook the excess on LPG: base case total Rs 52,106 a month versus battery route Rs 37,500 or so (Rs 35,252 plus losses) [I]. The battery route is only about Rs 14,600 a month better, yet the base capex of Rs 6.9 lakh costs about Rs 11,500 a month over five years [I] (6.9 lakh / 60). So the battery's edge over keeping two gas burners is roughly Rs 3,000 a month in the base and favourable cases (Rs 8,300 less Rs 5,200 amortisation in the favourable case) and negative in the conservative case (Rs 10,900 less Rs 22,000) [I].

## Verdict

Reject. Reasons, in order:
1. The deciding premise (the discom bills a 30 kW kitchen as a 20 kW consumer when a battery caps grid draw) has no official support; the one rule found says tariff follows sanctioned load, so the battery changes nothing billed [S7]. If wrong, the buyer loses the entire capex.
2. Most of the "Rs 3.5 lakh" is a tariff-structure play on unofficial numbers [S2]; the induction part is available to every cooker seller.
3. Battery costs are two to three times the hunter's per-kWh figure [S11, S12]; payback is 2-4 years even when everything works.
4. A gas-assist or load-staggering alternative nearly matches it [I].
5. Founder economics fail: no payback, cash need above capital [I].

Hold would only be justified if MSEDCL confirms in writing that a battery-capped connection stays in the 20 kW band. I would not spend on that unless the user wants to close the question; it is cheap (letter, see A2).

### Unchecked and top uncertainties

1. Official 2026 LT-II table and the rule on what decides the 20 kW band (could not open any official page).
2. Real peak kW of a one-cylinder eatery (average 9.5 kW; peak is assumed).
3. Whether commercial LPG returns to a low price, which removes the induction saving.
Not checked: GST and electricity duty on the bills, Pune commercial PNG tariff, fire NOC rules for batteries, power factor effects, landlord approval, BEE vendor list, actual eatery counts.
